Lakenridge and Westminster Ridge: Historical Research Dossier

Document type: Historical research compilation

Research status: Working dossier; sources checked through September 2026

Executive summary

The best-supported story is a two-stage suburban development.

  1. Westminster Park was already a named residential project by 1917. Period real-estate and building notices describe a plan for 52 houses and identify the Westminster Park Company, its president Norman W. Walker, architect Joseph J. Eberle, and contractor M. W. Walker.
  2. Westminster Ridge grew as a later, nearby subdivision on an older estate/woodland landscape. A 1939 Architectural Forum article says the site had once been an estate, that its original house still stood, and that local developer Fred P. Stafford began developing the subdivision around 1927.
  3. The Depression interrupted the first build-out. By 1930, about 30 higher-priced homes had been built. Construction then slowed sharply until County Housing Corporation, led by architect-builder Lee Perry, acquired about 80 vacant lots in a 40-acre tract and restarted construction in 1938.
  4. The lake was part of the subdivision’s identity before the modern Lakenridge Club. A 1938 trade article calls a small lake and playground part of the tract. In 1939, the lake was being purchased from the original subdividers by a community lake club. A 1910 Bromley map appears to show a waterbody in the same general location, labeled “Reservoir,” but that map-based identification is not conclusive proof of the present pond’s exact origin.
  5. Lakenridge is both a lake club and a neighborhood institution. Current club materials describe a private, resident-run club serving Westminster Ridge, with swimming, fishing, boating, a swim team, children’s programs, seasonal events, and lake stewardship. IRS-derived nonprofit records list Lakenridge Club as a 501(c)(7) recreation organization with a 1973 ruling/year-foundation date; that date should not be treated as the club’s original founding date without the underlying incorporation records.
  6. A. C. Todd is now tied to the landscape by a period county atlas. The 1901 White Plains plate labels a substantial tract “A. C. Todd.” A large early house at 20 Stafford Place is a plausible lead for the estate house mentioned in 1939, but its identity remains unproven.

The clearest narrative for the website is therefore: an early-20th-century Westchester residential experiment, interrupted by the Depression, revived as a carefully planned 1930s neighborhood, with its lake becoming the community’s shared center.

What was here before the neighborhood?

The larger White Plains landscape

White Plains grew from a rural colonial settlement into a suburban city. The City of White Plains says the area was known to the Weckquaesgeek as Quarropas, commonly translated as “white marshes” or “white plains.” In 1683, English settlers purchased 4,435 acres; a royal patent followed in 1721. The city’s official history records a population of only 505 in 1790 and 675 in 1820, when most of the land was still improved farmland. The railroad reached White Plains in 1844, the village incorporated in 1866, and White Plains became a city in 1916.

That context matters because Westminster Ridge was not built on an already urbanized block grid. It was part of the early suburban transformation of a landscape that had been rural, wooded, agricultural, and organized around large holdings and estates.

An estate, woods, and rolling land

The most useful description of the pre-subdivision site comes from the March 1939 issue of Architectural Forum. Its article on Westminster Ridge says the property had once been an estate and that the original house was still being used. It describes the surrounding land as rolling, heavily wooded, and visually open, with “big trees,” winding roads, and long views.

The article’s plot plan labels a large area “400 acres natural woods,” while the article’s text describes County Housing Corporation’s purchase as an 80-lot tract of approximately 40 acres. These figures may refer to different areas or may reflect a plan/printing inconsistency. They should be preserved as period wording rather than silently reconciled.

The neighborhood’s topography was important to its design. The 1939 article says the steep hills required special planning and that roads were laid out to follow the land. This is one reason the area feels unlike a flat, conventional street grid.

The lake before Lakenridge

There are three separate pieces of evidence about the waterbody:

  • A 1910 Bromley map of White Plains shows a waterbody beside Garretson Road labeled “RESERVOIR.” Its position appears close to the present Todds Pond, but this is a visual/map-based inference, not a definitive identification.
  • A 1938 American Builder article says a “small lake and playground” were included in the Westminster Ridge tract.
  • A 1939 Architectural Forum article says the lake was being purchased from the original subdividers by a community lake club, with member dues supporting equipment, beach sand, and lifeguards.

Together, these sources strongly suggest that the lake or reservoir predates the modern club and was central to the subdivision plan. They do not yet establish whether the pond is entirely natural, enlarged from a natural wetland, excavated, dammed, or fed by a spring. That question needs deeds, engineering plans, drainage records, or a local environmental history.

A. C. Todd and the map record

The strongest new lead on the land’s ownership comes from the 1901 Atlas of Westchester County. The White Plains plate labels a large tract “A. C. Todd” north and east of the later Westminster Ridge street pattern. This does not, by itself, prove that Todd owned every acre later used by Westminster Park or Westminster Ridge, but it gives the Todd connection a period map source rather than leaving it only in local tradition.

The map sequence also helps separate what is known from what is inferred:

  • The 1901 atlas identifies the Todd tract.
  • The 1910–11 county atlas labels the nearby waterbody “RESERVOIR” and shows a dam-like feature.
  • The 1929–31 atlas shows the reservoir beside a developing street pattern.
  • The 1940 Westchester County aerial shows a compact dark waterbody inside the curved-road neighborhood.

Taken together, these images make a persuasive visual case that the pond/reservoir persisted through the neighborhood’s transition from large landholdings to a built subdivision. They still do not settle the engineering question of whether the water was natural, enlarged, or fully constructed.

The possible original estate house

The 1939 Architectural Forum article says that the property had been an estate and that its original house was still standing during the Westminster Ridge build-out. A promising present-day research lead is 20 Stafford Place, a large Colonial-style house that public property records describe as built in 1907, approximately 4,012 square feet on 0.74 acres. Local street-history research says Stafford Place was renamed in 1934 to honor Fred P. Stafford, the developer associated with the later Westminster Ridge phase.

The house’s age, scale, and location make it a plausible candidate for the estate house mentioned in 1939. It is not yet proven to be that house. A deed chain, historic tax card, period directory entry, or a photograph explicitly tying the building to the original estate is still needed. The article therefore treats 20 Stafford Place as a clearly labeled lead, not as an established identification.

Names and place relationships

Several names appear in the sources and should be explained on the website:

  • White Plains: the city and historical setting.
  • Westminster Park: an earlier or adjacent residential development name documented in 1917 building notices.
  • Westminster Ridge: the neighborhood name used in the 1930s development articles and by residents today.
  • Todds Pond / Todd Pond: the waterbody name used by maps, local histories, and modern geographic references.
  • Lakenridge Club: the private lake club associated with the pond and Westminster Ridge.

A local-history account says that A. C. Todd created Westminster Park in 1912 and that the pond took its name from him. Another local-history page refers to the developer as Arthur Todd and says nearby streets were named for him. The 1917 Real Estate Record and Builders’ Guide confirms Westminster Park as a named development, but it does not, by itself, confirm the Todd biography or the exact origin of the pond’s modern name. Those details should be presented as local tradition unless a deed, plat, newspaper profile, or corporate filing is found.

The 1901 county atlas now provides an independent, period indication that A. C. Todd held a substantial tract in the relevant part of White Plains. It strengthens the connection between Todd and the landscape, but the exact relationship between that tract, the 1912 Westminster Park story, and the later Westminster Ridge subdivision remains a subject for deed and plat research.

Streets that were planned but not built

The 1929–31 county atlas shows Bernard Pl., Francis Pl., Colonial Place, and the northward road known as Stang Avenue beside the reservoir. It is a plat and tax atlas, so a street label shows the planned layout, not proof that every road opened.

The names also refer to different roads:

  • Colonial Place was built around 1908 and later became Stafford Place. It was briefly called Chadwick Place in January 1934 before being renamed Stafford Place in September 1934.
  • Colonial Terrace was the earlier name of the road that became Chadwick Road. Its northern extension, added around 1927, was first known as Stang Avenue. The extension became Colonial Terrace in 1933, and the whole road became Chadwick Road in 1934.
  • Bernard Place and Francis Place appear on a local list of White Plains paper streets. The list says they appeared on the official city map but were never built.

The old plan therefore shows a planned street relationship north of Colonial Place/Stafford Place, but the final link to Bernard Place was never completed as a through road. The local street history describes Chadwick Road as ending near the paper Bernard Place alignment. A current Westchester County parcel query lists a Francis Pl parcel under the City of White Plains. A 2012 report describes city-owned Francis Place parcels as parkland and discusses a city purchase of the wildlife corridor between Bernard Place and Chadwick Road, with the possibility of removing paper streets that were no longer needed for development.

This is the best-supported explanation for the gap visible between the old and current maps: the road network was partly built, while the remaining mapped streets were retained as paper alignments and later treated as open-space land. I have not yet found the formal city resolution that would establish whether Bernard Place or Francis Place was legally de-mapped.

Sources: Westchester County 1929–31 atlas; Stafford Place street history; Chadwick Road street history; White Plains paper-street list; 2012 open-space report.

How the neighborhood was built up

1. Suburban transportation made the area developable

White Plains was already connected to New York by rail by 1844. The New York, Westchester & Boston Railway added a more direct suburban-growth catalyst: construction began in 1909, and service began in 1912. The city’s Historic Preservation Commission says the line had a White Plains terminal and four stations in the city before service ended in 1937.

A 1912 article in Columbia University’s digitized Real Estate Record and Builders’ Guide described more than 50 residential tracts under development along the railway corridor. The railway did not necessarily run through Westminster Ridge itself, but it helped create the market and commuting logic that made wooded Westchester subdivisions attractive.

2. Westminster Park was being marketed by 1917

The January 13, 1917 issue of Real Estate Record and Builders’ Guide carried a short notice titled “New Westchester Development.” It said the American Town Planning Company and the Garden City Corporation planned 52 two-and-a-half-story frame and brick dwellings at Westminster Park, White Plains, from privately prepared plans, at an estimated expenditure of about $200,000.

The July 21, 1917 issue followed with a specific building notice:

  • Developer/owner: Westminster Park Company
  • President: Norman W. Walker
  • Architect: Joseph J. Eberle
  • Contractor: M. W. Walker
  • House: two-and-a-half-story frame and stucco dwelling
  • Estimated cost: $10,000

These notices provide an unusually good early paper trail. They show that the area was not an accidental collection of houses; it was being conceived and promoted as a planned residential development before the later Westminster Ridge build-out.

3. Fred P. Stafford’s 1920s subdivision

The 1939 Architectural Forum article says that Fred P. Stafford, described as president of a local laundry, developed Westminster Ridge “twelve years ago,” which places the start around 1927. Stafford built a home for himself on six hilltop lots.

The same article says that by 1930 approximately 30 houses had been built, with prices ranging from about $15,000 to $40,000. The development therefore began as a relatively expensive, custom or semi-custom neighborhood rather than as a low-cost tract.

A local-history account says that roads were constructed in 1928–29 and made public in 1932. This is plausible in the context of the period article, but the road dates still need confirmation from city street-acceptance records or subdivision filings.

4. The Depression stalled construction

The early momentum did not last. The 1939 article says that building stopped during the Depression and that, before Lee Perry’s involvement, only two or three lots had sold in the preceding two years. This explains why the neighborhood contains evidence of more than one development era: an early 1920s/early-1930s phase and a concentrated late-1930s revival.

5. Lee Perry and County Housing Corporation revived the tract

The 1939 article says that County Housing Corporation, with Lee Perry as president, purchased 80 vacant lots in a roughly 40-acre tract. Roads and utilities were already installed, which made it possible to restart construction quickly.

Perry was both an architect and builder. The article says he designed the houses, adapted the plans to the steep hills, and sold the homes with their lots. The target price was just under $6,000 to about $10,000, a sharp change from the earlier $15,000–$40,000 houses.

The article reports that the first new house was built in April 1938 and that approximately 30 houses were completed in ten months. The November 1938 American Builder gives a similar snapshot: 41 houses were built or under construction by September 1, 1938, with sales running ahead of completions. It places prices at roughly $5,800–$8,500.

The resulting neighborhood was marketed as attainable but not bare-bones: carefully planned roads, durable materials, good views, a private lake, and a playground. The period trade press presented Westminster Ridge as an example of how design and construction discipline could make modestly priced homes feel established and distinctive.

The lake and the Lakenridge Club

The community’s original recreation center

The 1938 American Builder article says that “a small lake and playground are included in the tract.” The 1939 Architectural Forum article is more specific: the lake was being purchased from the original subdividers by a community lake club.

The 1939 article describes an early operating model:

  • Member dues were $2 per month.
  • Dues paid for equipment, beach sand, and lifeguards.
  • Admission fees also helped finance the purchase of the lake.
  • The lake club was a neighborhood institution, not simply an amenity controlled by a single homeowner.

This is a valuable origin story for modern Lakenridge. It shows that shared stewardship, dues, swimming, and lake-centered social life were present at the beginning of the neighborhood’s second major build-out.

Modern club records

The club’s current public materials document continuity in the lake’s use, even if they do not supply the full founding history:

  • The membership packet describes opening-day and lake-cleanup traditions, the Kids Fishing Derby, an “Open the Lake” party, a swim team, and a Guppies program.
  • The regulations describe swimming, fishing, boating, catch-and-release rules, lifeguards, a raft test, and other managed-waterbody practices.
  • A 2017 lifeguard posting describes Lakenridge as a private lake club in the middle of White Plains whose members and board are neighbors in Westminster Ridge.
  • The club’s newsletters are distributed to Westminster Ridge houses, reinforcing the relationship between the lake and the surrounding neighborhood.

IRS-derived nonprofit profiles list Lakenridge Club Inc. as a 501(c)(7) social/recreation organization and give a 1973 ruling or year-founded date. That is useful evidence for the club’s formal tax-exempt history, but it should not be described as the date the lake club began unless the incorporation papers or an earlier club record confirm it.

Architecture and physical character

The period sources offer a surprisingly clear architectural story.

Early Westminster Park

The 1917 notices describe two-and-a-half-story frame and brick or frame-and-stucco houses. The scale suggests substantial early suburban homes, consistent with the higher 1930-era prices described later.

The Perry phase

Lee Perry’s 1938–39 houses were designed for lower prices but were still presented as architect-designed homes. The Architectural Forum describes Colonial-influenced designs, careful planning for steep sites, and prices from below $6,000 to $10,000. The American Builder article highlights models called “The Lee” and “The Westminster,” along with asbestos-cement shingles and other standardized construction choices.

Landscape as design

The neighborhood’s identity came from more than house style:

  • Roads curve through rolling terrain rather than following a rigid grid.
  • Existing trees were retained and used as a selling point.
  • Homes were placed to capture long views.
  • The lake and playground functioned as common amenities.
  • The development was close enough to rail and city services to support commuting, but it was marketed as a retreat-like landscape.

The 1939 plot plan and photographs are especially useful visual evidence because they show the developers’ intended relationship between house lots, winding roads, woods, and the lake.

People and notable residents

The historical record is stronger for developers and civic figures than for a complete list of homeowners. A good website should avoid publishing a raw roster of private residents and instead focus on people whose public roles are documented.

Developers, builders, and designers

  • Fred P. Stafford: Local laundry president and early Westminster Ridge developer. The 1939 article says he began developing the neighborhood around 1927 and built a home for himself on six hilltop lots.
  • Lee Perry: Architect-builder and president of County Housing Corporation. He led the 1938 revival, designed the economical houses, and adapted them to the hills.
  • Norman W. Walker: President of the Westminster Park Company in the 1917 building notice.
  • Joseph J. Eberle: Architect named in the 1917 Westminster Park construction notice.
  • M. W. Walker: Contractor named in the same notice.

Publicly documented former residents or community figures

  • Stephen Holden III: A New York State Senate resolution says Holden was born in White Plains and raised in Westminster Ridge. It honors his later work as a local attorney, civic leader, and supporter of historic preservation. He is the clearest publicly documented notable former resident found in this initial pass.
  • Pasquale “Pat” Barile: His obituary describes him as a long-time Lakenridge Club president and a White Plains community leader. He may be useful for a later oral-history or “people of the lake” feature, subject to family/source permissions.
  • Tom Roach: A 2010 local news report identifies him as having lived in Westminster Ridge while serving as White Plains acting mayor. This is a dated public reference, not proof of current residence.

Source notes and reliability

Strong sources

  • Period trade journals and building notices from 1917, 1938, and 1939.
  • Official City of White Plains history and Historic Preservation Commission pages.
  • Official U.S. Census publications.
  • Official USGS historical mapping.
  • Lakenridge’s own public membership, regulations, and lifeguard documents.
  • New York State Senate resolution for Stephen Holden III.

Useful but secondary sources

  • Local-history blogs about White Plains waterways and streets.
  • Resident-oriented real-estate descriptions.
  • Obituaries and local news reports.
  • IRS-derived nonprofit directories.

These are valuable leads and often preserve details not yet found in archives, but the final website should label them as local accounts, newspaper reports, obituaries, or nonprofit-directory data rather than presenting them as definitive institutional history.

Research questions still open

The following are the most promising next steps if the site wants a deeper, fully documented history:

  1. Find the original subdivision plat. Search Westchester County land records and White Plains planning/engineering files for Westminster Park, Westminster Ridge, Westminster Park Company, Westminster Ridge Realty Corporation, A. C. Todd, and Fred P. Stafford.
  2. Trace the pond’s legal ownership. The key deed would be the transfer from the original subdivider to the community lake club described in 1939.
  3. Determine the pond’s physical origin. Look for drainage plans, dam permits, old topographic maps, engineering reports, or environmental records. The sources currently prove an early waterbody/reservoir and a later managed lake, but not whether it was natural or constructed.
  4. Locate the 1930 Sanborn sheet covering the pond. It may show the feature’s label, shoreline, dam, nearby buildings, and road names.
  5. Search local newspapers. The White Plains Daily Reporter (1905–1941) and Daily Press (1929 onward) should contain subdivision advertisements, lake-club notices, road acceptance, opening events, and resident stories.
  6. Use city directories and census records to build a careful resident timeline. White Plains directories exist for 1928–29, 1931, 1932, 1934, 1937, 1942, 1947, 1950, and later years. Focus on public figures, long-term residents who consent to interviews, and broad demographic change rather than publishing private address lists.
  7. Interview older residents and former club officers. Ask specifically about the pond’s construction, the original clubhouse/beach, ice skating, winter hockey, the first swim team, early lake dues, and when the name Lakenridge became standard.
  8. Locate the neighborhood planning document mentioned by a local resident online. A Reddit discussion refers to an old Westminster Ridge planning document with a 1920s logo. It is a lead only; the document itself needs to be located and authenticated.

Suggested website framing

The history page can safely say:

Westminster Ridge grew from an early 20th-century Westchester suburban landscape of estates, woods, and rolling land. Westminster Park was being planned by 1917, and a later Westminster Ridge build-out began in the 1920s. The Depression paused construction, but architect-builder Lee Perry revived the neighborhood in 1938 with carefully designed, moderately priced homes on winding roads. The lake and playground were part of the plan from the beginning of that revival. By 1939, a community lake club was purchasing and managing the lake, establishing the shared traditions that continue through Lakenridge today.

That wording is supported by the period sources and avoids claiming more than the evidence currently proves.
